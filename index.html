Essay

Digital products often shift from useful tools into extractive businesses, yet that commercial drift never overrides user choice in an open market. In week 5 of our Visual Culture Seminar, Juliette separated design for sale from design for use. This collection follows my classmates thoughts across three phases that loosely mirror Cory Doctorow’s “enshittification”: solving a problem for use, exploiting attention for data and locking people in for recurring revenue. I agree that the shift is real, but I do not agree that it is final; in a free market, the user always decides.

In the initial for use stage, products must solve a clear problem to earn adoption, though commercial pressure often pushes successful tools toward adding performative features. Products that fail the first test of use are rejected swiftly, even after a polished launch. Natay puts that test plainly: good design “is not just about making something look appealing”; it should “solve a problem, support the user, and successfully do its job.” The Humane AI Pin failed that test and less than a year after it shipped, Humane ended sales and shut down its servers. Established products drift more gradually, adding updates just to appear innovative. Charitaa sees that saturation in Apple’s annual new features and in Instagram, which started with a clear “niche and an objective” before overloading its app with reels, stories, ads and shopping, losing its “essence”; shifting towards design for sale. Still, every product that abandons its core purpose leaves room for a better product to replace it.

In the for data stage, platforms deploy manipulative interfaces to capture attention and harvest personal information, yet users push back quickly by walking away or forcing reversals. Andrea’s excerpt from Sally Woellner shows why companies are “so desperate to get your time and attention”: keeping people on screen lets platforms gather behavioral data, sell it to data brokers and serve more ads. But when those tactics overwhelm the experience, users can be quick to leave. Comparing efficient Korean apps with endless pop-ups on Temu and Shein, Claire notes the clutter made her “leave the app.” Even when users stay, collective demand guides companies. In Hannah’s excerpt, after OpenAI replaced GPT-4o with GPT-5, loyal users “demanded that they bring back the personality of their beloved companions,” forcing OpenAI to restore the old model for paying users within days. Companies want to design for extraction, but users still control demand.

The for rent stage poses the strongest challenge to user choice, as dominant companies exploit market capture, buy up the competition and own the cloud infrastructure everyone else runs on, can easily lock customers in and raise prices. In class, Leila pointed out that just as Adobe and Uber started as an affordable service before turning expensive once they had captured the market, free AI tools now lock users in to (over-)charge later. Even when users resent how products worsen in the shift from use to sale, market concentration slows the exit. Linzhuo notes that on China’s cluttered platforms, “the dominance of major companies means there may be no suitable, simpler alternative,” leaving frustrated people “putting up with the clutter.” Deeper down the stack, Matias points to an AWS outage shutting down Canva and Duolingo alongside throttled Starlink Wi-Fi, raising our section’s recurring question: “what do we actually own? is everything rented now?” Doctorow goes furthest, noting that companies lock customers in when they “just buy all the competitors,” leaving “nowhere to go.”

Ultimately, lock-in reflects market structure rather than fate, so wherever competition stays open, user frustration still checks corporate power and shows designers what to build next. Even AI pricing hits economic limits: Jun already hears of companies rehiring laid-off engineers after finding AI costs more than their salaries. In software, lock-in makes leaving expensive, not impossible. Doctorow notes that people leave once “the cost of leaving is lower than the cost of staying,” so the fix is to “just make it easier for people to leave.” That opening let Figma challenge Adobe’s bloat, whereas infrastructure like AWS and Starlink requires regulatory oversight because designers cannot build a weekend rival to data centers. Where markets stay open, Valerie’s excerpt from Maciej Cegłowski urges us to “reclaim the web from technologists who tell us that the future they've imagined is inevitable, and that our role in it is as consumers.” Cegłowski treats “consumer” as passive, yet walking away is an active veto and every product lives or dies by whether people keep choosing it.

Doctorow quotes: Doctorow, Cory. “How to Save the Internet From ‘Enshittification.’” Interview by Bartolomeo Sala. Jacobin, October 14, 2025. https://archive.is/3D01J.
